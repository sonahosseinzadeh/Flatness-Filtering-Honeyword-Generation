\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This thesis started from a mismatch. Honeyword-based breach detection
depends on flatness, the promise that an attacker who has read the
password file cannot pick the real credential out of a sweetword set
with probability better than $1/k$, and every widely deployed honeyword
generator has been evaluated against attackers that no longer represent
the state of the art. \textcite{dani2024passfilter} showed that a small
character-level CNN, trained as a binary classifier on real-vs-decoy
pairs, breaks flatness on essentially every scheme they tested. That
left an open question: can flatness be restored against such an attacker
without redesigning the generator?

The answer we develop is yes, with qualifications. The same CNN
discriminator PassFilter used as an attack, reused unmodified as a
defensive oracle at storage time, gates sweetword sets against a small
set of measurable flatness conditions. Sets that fail are regenerated,
sets that pass are stored. Neither the generator nor the attacker
changes; only the pipeline combining them does. On the ten-method
benchmark of \Cref{ch:results}, the two-stage filter with a
multiplicative pre-screening band brings the attacker's
$\mathrm{ASR}@5$ down from around $0.75$ for the unfiltered baselines to
$0.29$--$0.34$ for the strongest configurations at
$N_{\text{train}} = 10000$, with the real password ranked near the
middle of the twenty candidates ($F_{\text{rank}} \approx 0.42$--$0.46$).
Under the thresholds adopted in \Cref{sec:method-threshold-tradeoff},
these ratio filters accept between $29\%$ and $78\%$ of passwords across
corpus sizes, well inside the deployable range and far above the
single-digit acceptance of the initial strict configuration.

\section{Summary of Contributions}

The contributions map to \Cref{ch:method} and \Cref{ch:results}. First,
an attacker-relative definition of flatness stated at the level of the
attacker-induced distribution over a sweetword set and characterized by
four measurable quantities: $F_{\text{rank}}$, $F_{\text{ent}}$, the top
gap, and an $\varepsilon$-flatness bound
$q_{\max} \le 1/k + \varepsilon_{\max}$ on the largest entry of that
distribution. Second, a hybrid flatness-filtering algorithm combining
candidate-level pre-screening with set-level rejection sampling, which
is generator-agnostic and requires retraining neither the generator nor
the attacker.

The benchmark is the third contribution: ten generation methods spanning
rule-based tweaking, representation-learning HoneyGen variants, and a
self-trained GAN, evaluated under a common protocol at three training
sizes and against three attacker classes. The HoneyGen-tuned CNN, a
mixed-corpus CNN trained on several decoy families, and a frequency
ranker together separate genuine flatness from single-attacker
artifacts.

\section{What the Numbers Actually Say}

Three quantitative findings are worth extracting from \Cref{ch:results}
and stating plainly for a reader in a hurry.

Unfiltered honeyword generators are highly distinguishable to the CNN.
All four baselines, Tweaking, Password-Model, Hybrid-Model, and HoneyGen
Baseline, report $\mathrm{ASR}@5$ in the range $0.68$--$0.77$ at every
training-set size we tested, about three times the random-guessing rate
of $0.25$.

Flatness filtering cuts $\mathrm{ASR}@5$ substantially, and the ratio
band does most of the work. At $N_{\text{train}} = 10000$ the
Prescreen-Ratio and Hybrid-Ratio variants push $\mathrm{ASR}@5$ down to
$0.341$ and $0.293$ with $F_{\text{rank}}$ near the ideal middle value
of $0.5$, which is genuine near-uniform ranking, while the additive
filters manage moderate reductions to the $0.51$--$0.61$ range. The
relative ordering of methods holds as the training corpus varies over
$2000$, $5000$, and $10000$. Because acceptance is treated as a
first-class deployability requirement (\Cref{sec:results-cost}), the
recommended configurations are the ratio filters read through the
two-stage criterion: the flattest methods among those that stay
deployable.

The GAN's apparent flatness is a distributional artifact and should not
be read as a defence. Following the CNN's top guesses, its
$\mathrm{ASR}@5$ at $N_{\text{train}} = 10000$ is essentially zero, but
$F_{\text{rank}}$ is $0.920$: the real password sits near position
twenty on the average set. The CNN treats the GAN's decoys as looking
\emph{more} like real passwords than the real password itself, so a
rational attacker inverts the ranking and reaches
$\mathrm{ASR}@5 = 0.872$, the value the two-sided rational-attacker
floor reports. That makes the GAN the weakest method in the benchmark
rather than the strongest. Two independent checks confirm bias rather
than flatness. Under the frequency attacker the same sets report
$\mathrm{ASR}@5 = 0.388$, the highest value in that column. Under the
mixed-corpus attacker, which has seen GAN decoys during training, no
inversion is needed at all: following the ranking in the ordinary
direction reaches $0.99$ at $N_{\text{train}} = 5000$, where the
HoneyGen-tuned CNN followed the same way manages only $0.071$. The
artifact sharpens with corpus size; at $N_{\text{train}} = 2000$ the
GAN's $F_{\text{rank}}$ sits closer to the middle, so the inversion
grows as the generator trains on more data.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion-limitations}

The framework has five limitations a deployment would need to reason
about explicitly.

\paragraph{Coverage below 100\%.}
The most important limitation for real-world use is that the
countermeasure does not protect every user. The filter stores a
sweetword set only when it can certify that set as flat; when it cannot
do so within the regeneration budget it gives up on that password, and
that account gets no flatness-guaranteed set. At the operating point of
this thesis the strongest ratio filters certify only between $29\%$ and
$78\%$ of passwords (\Cref{tab:accept}), leaving a large share of users,
in the worst case the majority, without the guarantee. A deployable
system has to protect $100\%$ of accounts; it cannot simply refuse to
store honeywords for the users it finds difficult. As it stands the
method would need pairing with a fallback for the rejected passwords, an
unfiltered set for instance, which weakens the guarantee for exactly
those users. Reaching near-total coverage without sacrificing flatness
is the single biggest obstacle to adoption, and we return to it as an
open question below.

\paragraph{Model-relative guarantees.}
The filter enforces flatness against one specific attacker, the
character-level CNN of \Cref{sec:method-cnn}. A different attacker, a
larger CNN, a transformer, or a discriminator using side information
beyond the password string, may recover distinguishability on sets the
filter has certified. The guarantees are best read as flatness against
this class of learned attacker rather than flatness in general.
\Cref{sec:results-freq} shows they transfer partially to the frequency
attacker, which is weaker; whether they transfer to stronger models is
an empirical question requiring each candidate attacker to be run
through the same benchmark.

\paragraph{One-sided set-level acceptance.}
The set-level acceptance test constrains only the maximum of the induced
distribution $q$. A set in which some honeyword receives an anomalously
low $q$ would pass even though $q$ is not close to uniform. In our data
that does not translate into ASR degradation, since ASR responds to the
top of the ranking rather than the tail, but the acceptance predicate
could be tightened to bound both ends of $q$. This is a small extension
of \Cref{alg:hff} and worth doing in a production build.

\paragraph{Acceptance-rate cost of the ratio filter.}
The strongest security in the benchmark, from the Prescreen-Ratio and
Hybrid-Ratio filters, comes at the lowest acceptance rates among the
filtered methods, between $29\%$ and $78\%$ across corpus sizes against
$65\%$--$97\%$ for the additive filters, and at a higher average number
of regeneration attempts, up to about $4.6$ per accepted set
(\Cref{tab:accept}). Under the relaxed thresholds this is no longer the
crippling single-digit acceptance of the initial configuration: every
filtered method clears the $25\%$ deployability mark at every training
size. The residual cost is real but falls entirely at storage time,
since filtering happens once per account for the lifetime of the
credential. A deployment that cannot afford the ratio filter's rejection
budget can move to an additive filter and recover most of the security
gain over the unfiltered baseline at a much higher acceptance rate.

\paragraph{Corpus and language.}
All experiments use the cleaned RockYou corpus, a leak of primarily
English-language, user-chosen web passwords from one consumer service.
Its distributional properties, long low-probability tails, common
suffixes, digit sequences at the end, are what the FastText embeddings,
the GAN, and the CNN all learn to model. Whether the same numbers hold
on corpora with different structure, such as enterprise credentials,
non-English leaks, or machine-generated passwords, is an empirical
question. Extending the benchmark to \emph{darkc0de} as a second corpus
is on the immediate next-steps list for this pipeline.

\section{Open Questions}

Beyond the immediate limitations, five research questions remain open.

The first is adversarial adaptation. As constructed, the filter is a
static defence: the oracle CNN is trained once and reused. An attacker
who knew the filter existed and had a sample of accepted sets could
train against those sets rather than against raw generator output.
Whether the generator, the oracle, and the attacker can be co-trained
toward a stable equilibrium at $\mathrm{ASR}@5 \approx 0.25$ is a
question this thesis does not resolve. The pieces are in place to run
the experiment; the answer requires running it.

The second is transferability across a class of attackers. The current
guarantees hold against one CNN. A more useful guarantee would cover a
class of models, say any CNN of bounded depth and width, or any linear
classifier over a fixed feature set. The filter could then use an
ensemble oracle discriminating against the worst-case member of the
class. \textcite{chakraborty2022hbat} discuss related notions in their
survey, and connecting them to the flatness formalism of
\Cref{sec:method-formalism} is a natural extension.

The third is the theoretical relationship between the flatness metrics.
$F_{\text{rank}}$, $F_{\text{ent}}$, and $\varepsilon$-flatness are
correlated but not equivalent, and the acceptance predicate uses all
three plus the top gap. It is not obvious that this is the smallest set
of conditions sufficient to bound $\mathrm{ASR}@t$, or that all three
are individually necessary. A cleaner characterization, an inequality
connecting $q_{\text{real}}$, $F_{\text{ent}}$, and expected
$\mathrm{ASR}@t$, would let the filter constrain fewer quantities
without giving up guarantees.

The fourth is the LLM-based generator line. The construction of
\Cref{sec:method-filter} is generator-agnostic and would apply unchanged
to sweetword sets produced by the chunk-manipulation and
Metropolis--Hastings language models reviewed in
\Cref{sec:related-llm}. Whether the resulting $\mathrm{ASR}@5$ curves
match, exceed, or fall below the ratio-filtered HoneyGen numbers
reported here is an experiment worth running.

The fifth is coverage. As the limitations stress, the filter protects
only the passwords for which it can certify a flat set, and at the
current operating point that fraction is well below $100\%$. A practical
countermeasure has to cover every account, so the question is how to
reach near-total coverage without weakening flatness. Possible routes
include stronger or password-specific generators that produce flat
candidates more reliably, thresholds adapted per password to the hardest
cases, or a principled fallback for the residual passwords no generator
can flatten. Which of these, or which combination, closes the gap is the
most important question this thesis leaves open.

\section{Closing}

Honeywords are a modest defence with an outsized property: they turn a
silent failure, offline password recovery from a stolen hash file, into
an observable event at the authentication server. That property is worth
preserving as attacker capabilities improve, and the argument here is
that each new class of attacker should be reused as an enforcement tool
at storage time. The classifier that would otherwise recover the real
password becomes the gatekeeper that keeps any sweetword set with a
recoverable real password from being stored. The reversal is small and
the numbers are moderate, but the direction feels right: as long as
attackers keep getting better, defenders can keep the same tools,
pointed the other way.
