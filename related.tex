\chapter{Related Work}
\label{ch:related}

The literature on honeyword-based authentication runs along two
threads. The first is the design of \emph{generators} that produce
honeywords meant to be hard to tell apart from real passwords. The
second, more recent, is the design of \emph{distinguishers} that try to
break flatness under stronger attacker models than generators are
usually tested against. Both feed into this thesis: the ten generators
of \Cref{sec:method-gens} come from the first thread, the CNN of
\Cref{sec:method-cnn} from the second. This chapter reviews both, and
\Cref{sec:related-position} describes how the filtering construction of
\Cref{sec:method-filter} sits between them.

\section{Heuristic Honeyword Generators}
\label{sec:related-heuristic}

The earliest generators are the four \emph{chaffing} constructions
proposed alongside the primitive by \textcite{juels2013honeywords}:
\emph{chaffing-by-tweaking}, \emph{chaffing-by-tail-tweaking},
\emph{chaffing-with-a-password-model}, and
\emph{chaffing-with-tough-nuts}. Chaffing-by-tweaking applies
character-class substitutions to the real password to produce visually
similar decoys; chaffing-by-tail-tweaking restricts those edits to a
short suffix. Chaffing-with-a-password-model draws candidates from an
external password model such as a PCFG or a Markov chain, so the decoys
look like plausible passwords in the aggregate rather than like edits of
the real one. Chaffing-with-tough-nuts inserts random, high-entropy
strings that no attacker would submit at login and that therefore work
as guaranteed alarms.

These were evaluated against attackers that scored candidates
independently, typically the PCFG rankers of \textcite{weir2009pcfg} and
the Markov rankers of \textcite{narayanan2005fast}. Under that
evaluation model, carefully tuned chaffing schemes report flatness close
to $1/k$, which is what motivated the follow-up literature. Two of the
constructions, chaffing-by-tweaking and chaffing-with-a-password-model,
appear in our benchmark (\Cref{sec:method-gens}) as reference points
against the more sophisticated generators below, together with a hybrid
chaffing method that mixes the two and that we introduce for comparison.

The main criticism of the heuristic generators is
\emph{per-password plausibility}. Each decoy is designed to look like a
plausible password on its own, with no constraint on how the decoys look
\emph{as a set}. A generator can produce $k-1$ plausible but
distributionally clustered decoys that leave the real password
stranded, and the attacker need only notice the cluster to spot the
outlier. Later generators, and the filter of \Cref{sec:method-filter},
are built to address this.

\section{Representation-Learning Approaches: HoneyGen}
\label{sec:related-honeygen}

\textcite{dionysiou2021honeygen} propose \emph{HoneyGen}, which combines
a FastText password model with rule-based tweaking. The password model
produces nearest-neighbour candidates for the real password in the
FastText embedding space (\Cref{sec:bg-fasttext}), while a tweaking
module produces further candidates by character-class substitution. The
two streams are merged, pruned with a frequency-based filter that
removes overly common candidates, and the top $k-1$ are kept as
honeywords.

The original paper evaluates against frequency-based and PCFG-based
attackers, and HoneyGen improves on the chaffing baselines. Against that
class of attacker the conclusion is uncontroversial: representation
learning captures more of the password distribution than rules or PCFGs
alone, and the resulting decoys are harder to separate under a ranker of
the kind those attackers use.

Our position on HoneyGen is specific. We reuse the generator unmodified
as the candidate source for six of the ten methods in the benchmark
(HoneyGen Baseline, Set-Filter, Prescreen-Add, Prescreen-Ratio,
Hybrid-Add, Hybrid-Ratio). What we do not reuse is the paper's
evaluation: our attacker is the CNN of \Cref{sec:bg-cnn} rather than a
frequency ranker, and the flatness scores we measure under it are far
worse than the ones originally reported (\Cref{ch:results}). The
filtered variants are how we recover flatness against that stronger
attacker without touching the generator.

\section{Large-Language-Model Approaches}
\label{sec:related-llm}

More recent work uses large language models as the candidate source.
The chunk-level construction of \textcite{yu2023honeychunk} has drawn
particular attention: a password is disassembled into semantic chunks
that a pre-trained language model perturbs one at a time, and candidate
sets are drawn with Metropolis--Hastings sampling, an accept-or-reject
scheme that steers candidates toward regions the model assigns a high
real-password probability. Locality-sensitive hashing, which maps
similar strings to the same bucket, then drops near-duplicates from the
pool. These are among the most linguistically sophisticated generators
the honeyword literature has produced, and they report clear gains in
semantic plausibility over both HoneyGen and GAN-based methods.

The evaluation in this line, though, follows the same statistical
plausibility paradigm as the earlier work. Flatness is measured against
ranker-style attackers, and the reported metrics are distributional
distances rather than the success rate of a neural distinguisher trained
to identify the real password. The gap this leaves is the one
\textcite{dani2024passfilter} demonstrated for HoneyGen: a generator can
be indistinguishable under one attacker class and break under a stronger
one. We do not evaluate an LLM-based generator here, since our focus is
the reuse of the neural distinguisher as a defensive oracle rather than
the generation side. The construction of \Cref{sec:method-filter} is
agnostic to where candidates come from and would apply to an LLM-based
generator unchanged; \Cref{ch:conclusion} returns to this.

\section{GAN-Based Honeyword Generators}
\label{sec:related-gan}

\textcite{hitaj2019passgan} adapt the generative adversarial network of
\textcite{goodfellow2014generative} to password modelling in the attack
setting: the goal is guess sequences that recover more of a leaked
corpus than PCFG or Markov models manage at the same budget. The
adaptation is to train the generator against a discriminator that tries
to separate generated strings from real leaked ones, with both networks
working on character sequences.

GANs have already been studied as a honeyword source.
\textcite{fauzi2020passgan} analyse a game in which the defender uses
the generator of a PassGAN model to produce honeywords while the
attacker uses its discriminator to pick out the real password, and
assess how feasible PassGAN is as a generation strategy. We include a
GAN in the same spirit as one of the benchmark generators: a model that
has learned the password distribution should, in principle, produce
decoys that look real. Ours is a character-level generator trained from
scratch on the same corpus as the other generators (a \emph{self-trained}
GAN), and we sample decoys from it directly. GANs are prone to
\emph{mode collapse}, where the generator emits only a narrow set of
outputs instead of covering the variety of the training distribution;
for passwords that shows up as the same few common strings appearing
over and over. To get enough distinct decoys anyway, we oversample the
generator, drawing roughly five times as many candidates as a set needs,
and deduplicate the pool before assembling each sweetword set. This GAN
is one of the ten generators evaluated in the benchmark
(\Cref{ch:method}).

The theoretical case for a GAN as a honeyword generator is subtle, and
our results show it does not hold in practice. A generator that had
truly converged to the real password distribution would produce
indistinguishable decoys almost by definition. But real password
distributions have long low-probability tails that GANs are known to
underrepresent, so the decoys cluster in the high-probability region and
the real password, itself often a low-probability string, is left
looking anomalous. \Cref{ch:results} shows exactly that: under the CNN
attacker the GAN's real password is pushed to the bottom of the ranking
($F_{\text{rank}} \approx 0.92$), so its apparently low attack success
is an artifact of that inversion rather than genuine flatness, and a
mixed-corpus attacker exposes it. The GAN's flatness numbers have to be
read with care rather than taken at face value.

\section{Neural Distinguishers as Attackers: PassFilter}
\label{sec:related-passfilter}

The work most directly relevant to this thesis is PassFilter
\parencite{dani2024passfilter}. It trains a character-level
convolutional neural network as a binary classifier that predicts,
given a candidate password, whether it is real or a honeyword. The
network is small by modern standards, two convolutional layers over
character embeddings followed by a fully connected layer, and it is
trained on labelled pairs of real passwords and honeywords produced by
existing generators.

The paper's main result is that this classifier violates flatness on
every honeyword scheme it tests, including HoneyGen and its variants,
across multiple leaked corpora. The attacker success rate runs well
above $1/k$ throughout, sometimes by an order of magnitude, and the
ranking the classifier induces concentrates on the real password with a
probability the classical flatness definition rules out.

Using a learned discriminator as the attacker is not unique to
PassFilter. As noted in \Cref{sec:related-gan},
\textcite{fauzi2020passgan} already turn the discriminator of a PassGAN
model against its own generator's honeywords. PassFilter reads as the
general form of the same idea: a classifier trained to separate real
passwords from honeywords whatever generator produced them. Both
establish the neural distinguisher as the attacker class honeyword
schemes must withstand. This thesis takes that distinguisher and moves
it to the defensive side.

Our reading of the PassFilter result is that the honeyword literature
has been evaluating flatness against too weak an attacker class. The
constructive response we develop is to admit the same class of
classifier into the threat model, formalize flatness against it
(\Cref{sec:method-formalism}), then reuse the classifier itself, without
architectural change, as the enforcement mechanism during sweetword
construction (\Cref{sec:method-filter}). We call this reuse a
\emph{defensive oracle} to underline that the same object is used
offensively in PassFilter and defensively here; only the surrounding
pipeline changes.

Two aspects of the PassFilter setup differ from ours in ways that
matter. The paper releases model weights trained on a specific corpus
and uses them to attack sweetwords produced by generators trained on
that same corpus. We do not reuse those weights; we train our own CNN on
a disjoint attacker corpus (\Cref{sec:method-corpora}), which raises the
baseline attacker success rate but removes any shared-parameter bias
between attacker and generator. The paper also evaluates flatness at the
level of individual sets, whereas we report the full ASR curve
$\mathrm{ASR}(t)$ over the guess budget $t$, computed over the accepted
sets, for every generator under a common attacker.

\section{The HBAT Survey}
\label{sec:related-survey}

\textcite{chakraborty2022hbat} survey the honeyword literature and
propose a framework, \emph{honeyword-based authentication techniques}
(HBAT), that organizes existing schemes along three axes: generation
method, distributed security model, and evaluation metric. Their
treatment of flatness is compatible with, and formalizes, the definition
given by \textcite{juels2013honeywords}; they extend it to
$\varepsilon$-flatness and connect it to the two further security
notions mentioned in \Cref{sec:bg-flatness}.

Their attacker taxonomy runs from frequency-based rankers through PCFG
and Markov models to neural language models. Neural
\emph{distinguishers} of the kind introduced by
\textcite{dani2024passfilter} appear implicitly in the survey's
discussion of adversarial machine learning but are not folded into the
flatness definition itself. The refinement in
\Cref{sec:method-formalism} can be read as that integration.

\section{Position of This Thesis}
\label{sec:related-position}

The construction developed here sits at the intersection of the two
threads. From the generator thread it inherits the ten methods of
\Cref{sec:method-gens} unmodified. From the distinguisher thread it
inherits the CNN classifier of \textcite{dani2024passfilter}, again
without architectural change. What is new is the pipeline combining
them: the classifier is trained on a disjoint corpus and reused as the
oracle deciding which sweetword sets get stored, and the flatness
conditions it enforces are stated over the set-wide induced distribution
of \Cref{sec:method-formalism} rather than over individual candidates.

Three observations situate the contribution.
%
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Prior generators are evaluated against attackers they
    themselves inspired. Frequency-based generators are evaluated
    against frequency rankers, representation-learning generators
    against Markov or PCFG rankers. This thesis holds the attacker class
    fixed, at the CNN of \textcite{dani2024passfilter}, and lets the
    generators vary; that common attacker is what makes the ten methods
    comparable on a single ASR axis. Robustness to the choice of
    attacker is examined separately (\Cref{sec:results-freq}) by
    re-evaluating the accepted sets against a mixed-corpus CNN and a
    frequency ranker.

    \item Prior work on neural distinguishers stops at demonstrating
    that flatness is violated, without proposing a construction that
    restores it. This thesis proposes one and measures how far it gets.

    \item Filtering ideas already exist in the honeyword literature, but
    they operate at the candidate level with non-learned filters such as
    frequency pruning and dictionary matching. The two-stage filter of
    \Cref{sec:method-filter} uses a learned filter and, in its set-level
    stage, constrains the distribution rather than the individual
    candidates. That combination is what lets it enforce
    $\varepsilon$-flatness in the sense of \Cref{eq:eps-flat}.
\end{enumerate}
%
The rest of the thesis develops these three points.
\Cref{ch:method} specifies the construction and \Cref{ch:results}
measures its effect on ASR across the ten generators.
