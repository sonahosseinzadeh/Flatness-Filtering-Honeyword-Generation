\begin{abstract}
Honeyword schemes defend password databases against offline dictionary
attacks by storing decoy passwords, or sweetwords, alongside the real
one, so that a stolen hash file cannot be used without risking
detection. Their security rests on flatness: the real password must be
indistinguishable from its decoys. \textcite{dani2024passfilter} have
shown that a deep-learning classifier can pick the real password out of
a sweetword set, which breaks flatness for the honeyword generators in
current use.

This thesis reuses that classifier as a defence. A CNN flatness oracle
scores each candidate sweetword set and rejects the ones in which the
real password stands out, regenerating until a set passes or a
regeneration cap is reached. I benchmark ten generation methods on the
RockYou corpus: rule-based chaffing, HoneyGen variants, and a
self-trained GAN, at training sizes of 2000, 5000, and 10000. Each is
evaluated against three attackers, a HoneyGen-tuned CNN, a mixed-corpus
CNN, and a frequency ranker. The metric throughout is the attacker's
success rate at guess budget $t$ ($\mathrm{ASR}@t$), the fraction of
accounts compromised within $t$ guesses against the $k = 20$ sweetwords
of an account. Random guessing gives $t/k$.

The unfiltered generators are easy for the CNN to break. At $k = 20$
they reach $\mathrm{ASR}@1$ of $0.28$--$0.35$, $\mathrm{ASR}@5$ of
$0.68$--$0.76$, and $\mathrm{ASR}@10$ of $0.86$--$0.91$, against random
baselines of $0.05$, $0.25$, and $0.50$. Ratio-based filtering is the
strongest defence that remains deployable, cutting those figures to
$0.05$--$0.07$, $0.29$--$0.34$, and $0.58$--$0.63$, and leaving the real
password near the middle of the attacker's ranking. Reaching that point
means treating the acceptance rate as a deployment requirement rather
than a diagnostic.

The GAN deserves a warning. Its reported $\mathrm{ASR}@5$ is $0.87$,
which looks nothing like the near-zero value a naive CNN attacker
measures. The reason is that the real password sits at the bottom of the
ranking ($F_{\text{rank}} \approx 0.92$), so an attacker who inverts the
ranking recovers it almost every time. A mixed-corpus attacker pushes
the same sets close to $1$. What the GAN produces is inverted
distinguishability rather than flatness.


\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Honeywords, passwords, authentication,
flatness, deep learning.
\end{abstract}
